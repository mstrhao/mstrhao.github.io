Research interests

I investigate how the brain organizes sensory information into conscious experience, and how internal models and current context shape the content of that experience.

Conscious content
How does sensory input become a structured conscious experience?
Internal models
How do learned structures and current context shape what we experience?
Neural mechanisms
What neural dynamics and interactions support the formation and transformation of conscious content?

Projects

Four questions organize my research directions in consciousness, perception, and working memory.

Formation and organization of content

How does sensory input combine with internal order, relations, and task structure to form organized content representations?

Neural dynamics and changes in consciousness

How do the temporal dynamics of neural activity relate to changes in perception, working memory, and conscious state? This direction aims to connect behavioral measurements with neural recordings to examine these relationships.

Content representations across scales

How does neural activity at different scales support the formation and organization of content? This direction aims to connect questions at population, cellular, and dendritic scales and explore relationships between levels of representation.

Causal tests and content-related behavior

Can changing content-related neural activity alter corresponding behavior? This direction aims to combine recording and intervention to test causal mechanisms of content organization.

Methods

Psychophysics and behavior

Perceptual judgments and sequence working-memory tasks connect stimulus manipulations with how information is accessed, organized, and maintained.

Imaging and neural recording

My research draws on human high-field MRI, MEG, and EEG; macaque electrophysiology and two-photon imaging; and human stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) and single-unit recording.

Planned collaborative extensions

Collaborative directions plan to explore voltage imaging and macaque optogenetics to extend neural measurements and causal tests.

Developing computational approaches

Computational modeling is an area I am interested in developing further, using experimentally grounded questions to connect behavioral and neural data with explicit, testable predictions.

Foundations in published work

Past collaborative studies used brightness and visual-illusion paradigms, time-resolved neural measurements, and spatially and depth-resolved imaging, providing methodological foundations for current research. The published COGITATE study protocol, to which I contributed, proposed preregistered tests of competing theories and multimodal measurements.

2026-10-16

How Do We “See” Our Own Thoughts?

Sometimes we suddenly recognize a connection between two things, realize that a judgment is wrong, or have a thought we cannot yet put into words. None of these requires a mental picture. “Seeing” our thoughts may point to a more fundamental question: how does activity within a system become meaningful content that the subject experiences?

I want to approach this through internal models. By a model, I mean a capacity to organize objects, events, and relations. Thinking “this happened before that,” for example, involves placing two events in a directed relation. The same information can have different meanings when organized by different internal models. My proposal is that the organization formed by the currently recruited model helps constitute the content we experience. Explaining this proposal requires specifying which information is linked, which roles its elements occupy, and how those relations are formed in neural activity.

Once content has formed, does another inner “me” need to read it for experience to occur? That account invites a further question: how does the reader experience anything? I would first examine how far the organization and unfolding of content can take the explanation. A substantive question remains: what distinguishes a system using a relation in a computation from a subject experiencing that relation? Calling an internal state “experienceable” does not answer it.

We can make the questions more concrete. How does changing a model’s relational structure change experienced content? Which changes in its operation would leave relevant information available without that information being experienced? Answering requires comparing judgments about content, behavior, and neural activity, while distinguishing changes in content from changes in how an answer is produced. Perhaps “seeing a thought” is less about finding a pair of eyes inside the brain than understanding the organization and activity that constitute a particular thought and its experience.

References

  1. Mante et al. (2013). Context-dependent computation by recurrent dynamics in prefrontal cortex. Nature, 503, 78–84. DOI ↗

    Neural population activity and recurrent-network modeling explain how context changes information selection and integration; the study concerns choice computations rather than a direct test of conscious experience.

  2. King, Pescetelli & Dehaene (2016). Brain Mechanisms Underlying the Brief Maintenance of Seen and Unseen Sensory Information. Neuron. DOI ↗

    Comparing the brief maintenance of information reported as seen or unseen provides an experimental approach to distinguishing information availability from experienced content; visibility was assessed through participants’ reports.

2026-10-16

Does Finding a Representation Explain Consciousness?

If we can decode what someone is thinking from their neural activity, have we found the mechanism that makes the thought conscious? I think there is still a gap. Identifying which activity corresponds to which content differs from explaining how that content forms, persists, and changes. Treating a pattern at one moment as the answer may leave out the process through which the content arises.

A thought can remain relatively stable for several seconds without the neural activity supporting it standing still. In a working-memory study, Murray and colleagues found that stable population coding could coexist with substantial neural dynamics. This raises a question for me: which part of the process does a stable representation capture? What does it preserve, and which changes fall outside that description?

Imagine two processes from which we ultimately decode the same content, despite different paths to its formation. Matching decoding results do not establish identical states of the whole system, or identical experiences. Could the recruitment of an internal model and the unfolding formation of relations also be constitutive of experience? Examining only the pattern that remains may not settle the question.

Replacing “representation” with “dynamics” does not itself provide an explanation. We need to specify which changes to a process prevent content from forming, disrupt its maintenance, or transform it into something else. Memory can participate in these processes, but retaining information does not by itself explain a subject’s present experience and thought. I would rather investigate consciousness through the unfolding formation of content than look only for its counterpart at a particular instant.

References

  1. Murray et al. (2017). Stable population coding for working memory coexists with heterogeneous neural dynamics in prefrontal cortex. PNAS, 114, 394–399. DOI ↗

    Stable population coding can coexist with changing neural activity; this study investigates working-memory coding rather than directly testing conscious experience.

  2. Stokes et al. (2013). Dynamic Coding for Cognitive Control in Prefrontal Cortex. Neuron, 78, 364–375. DOI ↗

    Dynamic coding offers an account of how context organizes subsequent processing, illustrating an approach that examines a process rather than a single moment.
