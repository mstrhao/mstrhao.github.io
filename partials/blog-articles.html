Consciousness as a Whirlpool: What Kind of Process Is Experience?

A whirlpool is not an extra substance added to water. Water continually flows in and out, yet the whirlpool can persist as long as the organization of the flow is maintained. The same water, flowing differently, may no longer form a whirlpool. It is neither reducible to a particular drop nor something that exists independently of water. It is an organized motion that the water is undergoing.

Applied to consciousness, this analogy suggests a hypothesis: experience is not something stored inside a neuron, nor an extra layer of existence added to neural activity. When neural activity is organized in a particular way and actually unfolds, that process itself is experience.

But an analogy is not an explanation. We can describe precisely what kind of flow constitutes a whirlpool, yet we cannot describe with comparable precision what organization of neural activity constitutes the experience of red or of pain. Being organized is not enough: whirlpools, flames, and working machines are all organized, but that does not mean they are all conscious.

A whirlpool points to a possible way of existing: a phenomenon can be real without being an additional entity. Could consciousness be like this? If so, exactly what kind of physical process constitutes conscious experience?

How Do We “See” Our Own Thoughts?

Sometimes we suddenly recognize a connection between two things, realize that a judgment is wrong, or have a thought we cannot yet put into words. None of these requires a mental picture. “Seeing” our thoughts may point to a more fundamental question: how does activity within a system become meaningful content that the subject experiences?

I want to approach this through internal models. By a model, I mean a capacity to organize objects, events, and relations. Thinking “this happened before that,” for example, involves placing two events in a directed relation. The same information can have different meanings when organized by different internal models. My proposal is that the organization formed by the currently recruited model helps constitute the content we experience. Explaining this proposal requires specifying which information is linked, which roles its elements occupy, and how those relations are formed in neural activity.

Once content has formed, does another inner “me” need to read it for experience to occur? That account invites a further question: how does the reader experience anything? I would first examine how far the organization and unfolding of content can take the explanation. A substantive question remains: what distinguishes a system using a relation in a computation from a subject experiencing that relation? Calling an internal state “experienceable” does not answer it.

We can make the questions more concrete. How does changing a model’s relational structure change experienced content? Which changes in its operation would leave relevant information available without that information being experienced? Answering requires comparing judgments about content, behavior, and neural activity, while distinguishing changes in content from changes in how an answer is produced. Perhaps “seeing a thought” is less about finding a pair of eyes inside the brain than understanding the organization and activity that constitute a particular thought and its experience.

Does Finding a Representation Explain Consciousness?

If we can decode what someone is thinking from their neural activity, have we found the mechanism that makes the thought conscious? I think there is still a gap. Identifying which activity corresponds to which content differs from explaining how that content forms, persists, and changes. Treating a pattern at one moment as the answer may leave out the process through which the content arises.

A thought can remain relatively stable for several seconds without the neural activity supporting it standing still. In a working-memory study, Murray and colleagues found that stable population coding could coexist with substantial neural dynamics. This raises a question for me: which part of the process does a stable representation capture? What does it preserve, and which changes fall outside that description?

Imagine two processes from which we ultimately decode the same content, despite different paths to its formation. Matching decoding results do not establish identical states of the whole system, or identical experiences. Could the recruitment of an internal model and the unfolding formation of relations also be constitutive of experience? Examining only the pattern that remains may not settle the question.

Replacing “representation” with “dynamics” does not itself provide an explanation. We need to specify which changes to a process prevent content from forming, disrupt its maintenance, or transform it into something else. Memory can participate in these processes, but retaining information does not by itself explain a subject’s present experience and thought. I would rather investigate consciousness through the unfolding formation of content than look only for its counterpart at a particular instant.

Does Reporting “Unseen” Imply Unconscious Working Memory?

I remain skeptical of the interpretation of “unconscious working memory.” Studies by Trübutschek, Dehaene, and colleagues found that the locations of stimuli reported as unseen could still be reported above chance several seconds later; even two locations and their temporal order left usable information (2017; 2019). A recent multisite replication again shows that this behavioral phenomenon deserves serious attention. But a reliable result does not imply a unique explanation. King and Dehaene (2014) showed that subjective visibility and objective discrimination can draw on the same internal evidence yet produce different answers through different categorization and decision boundaries. My further interpretation is that the two tasks depend on different but coupled internal models: they do not merely read out content, but help organize what is selected, maintained, and used. One report of “unseen” therefore cannot, without further testing, directly establish that the content maintained for another task is unconscious. The 2017 study did use MEG to test the explanation of consciously maintaining a guess, but whether those neural indicators cover different forms of conscious representation remains a crucial premise. Notably, the same team’s 2019 mental-rotation study found representational reactivation and consciousness-related signals during manipulation, leading the authors to emphasize the limits of unconscious processing. What needs explaining is not simply how someone could perform correctly without seeing, but what content subsequently formed and was maintained, and on what grounds that process is judged unconscious. Until these explanations are clarified, moving directly from delayed-judgment ability to “unconscious working memory” still makes an inferential leap.

Does Changing Visual Experience Reveal What Constitutes It?

The 2024 Perceptography study proposed an interesting reverse search: train monkeys to judge whether optogenetic stimulation of inferior temporal cortex (IT) occurred, then seek image changes that prompt a “stimulation present” response even without stimulation. This is somewhat like looking for a needle in a haystack, but behavioral feedback gives the search direction and constrains the visual changes induced by stimulation. Yet finding images that are behaviorally confusable does not amount to fully reconstructing the monkey’s experience: different perceptual cues or judgment strategies can produce the same choice. I am more concerned with another possibility: stimulating IT may simply change the sensory evidence supplied by a relatively high-level visual area, while remaining a precursor to conscious processing. Even if the eventual experience changes, that does not establish that the stimulated activity itself constitutes experience. IT may, of course, participate in the recurrent organization that constitutes experience, but these possibilities need to be distinguished rather than inferred directly from an area’s hierarchical level. A more valuable next test is whether changing specific feedback or coupling relations, while content components remain present, produces predicted changes in their assignment, relations, or continuity. We need to move gradually from “stimulation affected experience” to “which organizational relations contribute to constituting experience.” The bridge between causal influence and experiential constitution cannot be skipped.

What Units Does the Brain Use to Organize Information?

Understanding the brain means not only identifying the algorithms it executes, but also asking: what units does it operate on? The basic physical units need not be the basic cognitive units.

Lin Chen’s “global-first” view reminds us that an object need not be assembled feature by feature from local properties such as color and shape. Instead, the object’s overall organization may first determine what those properties belong to. Through chunking, Chen emphasizes that the units of cognitive processing can be organized wholes rather than isolated items. A recent commentary further points out that such organization can improve retention of the whole, but may also come at the cost of precision for individual items.

Liping Wang’s team’s research on sequence working memory brings the relationship between content and structure into measurable neural representations: ABC and CBA contain the same items, but the items play different roles in the sequence. How this content is maintained in ordinal-position-related subspaces, placed into the appropriate positions according to rules, and rearranged can all become concrete experimental questions. These findings do not directly establish “global-first” processing, but they offer an opportunity to test how wholes and components constrain one another.

Following this line, I want to ask a further question: does learning not only change answers, but also gradually form reusable cognitive units, relational roles, and rules of operation? This remains an idea to be tested. Yet it moves the question beyond a general discussion of whether the brain is like a computer, toward how the brain forms content and structures that it can operate on.

From Neural Activity to Conscious Experience: How Can We Build the Bridge?

If experience is an organized physical process, consciousness research needs to explain why that organization corresponds to particular contents, relations, and temporal structures.

For example, seeing “a red circle and a blue square” and seeing “a blue circle and a red square” involve the same colors and shapes, yet the experiences differ because the assignment of colors has changed. Merely finding that the brain encodes red, blue, circles, and squares does not explain how these components form the objects before us. Suppose that interactions between certain neural populations determine this assignment. A model should then predict in advance: while color and shape information remains intact, changing which relation would make the red originally belonging to the circle be experienced as belonging to the square? Would restoring that relation restore the original assignment?

The same model of organization should also explain which properties of experience remain unchanged and which change when the input, task rules, or source of content changes. This is a route that remains to be tested. It may not eliminate the entire explanatory gap at once, but it can make a theory take on specific explanatory responsibilities: which organizational relations correspond to which experiential structures, and how experience should change when organization changes in a particular way. Moving from “activity correlates with experience” toward such causal correspondences is how we can build the bridge, section by section.

Why Do Our Feelings Change Before the Situation Does?

The same difficult situation can feel suffocating when we think about it alone, yet after talking with someone we trust, it may seem possible to face it again. The situation is still there, the deadline has not moved, and the difficulty has not disappeared on its own. Why have our feelings changed first?

Perhaps what we experience is never just the situation itself, but also its relationship to us: what it means to me, whether I can cope, whether I have to bear it alone, and whether other possibilities remain. Saying “I’ll think it through with you” does not solve the problem, but it may change something important: this is no longer mine alone to face.

So “the situation hasn’t changed” does not mean that nothing has changed. Someone’s willingness to listen and help is itself a change in our circumstances. A problem that felt like a wall may begin to offer somewhere to start.

Some experiments make this change concrete. When facing the threat of electric shock, holding a partner’s hand can reduce some threat-related brain responses; when viewing negative images, reinterpreting the situations they depict can also reduce subjective negative feelings. The threat or image remains, but its relationship to the person, and the way it is understood, has changed. Coan et al., 2006; Ochsner et al., 2002

This invites a further question: might emotion be more than a layer of feeling added after cognition, participating instead in how the world before us is organized? When we are tense, our attention keeps returning to what might go wrong; when we feel supported, options we could not previously see may come back into view. How we understand our circumstances affects emotion; emotion in turn changes which information becomes important and which actions seem possible. Bodily fatigue, tension, and relaxation also participate in this process.

This reminds me of the “motivational engine and content network” discussed earlier. Emotion may change both the motivation for action and the content toward which that motivation is directed: not just whether we have the energy to continue, but why we continue and where we go next.

What is truly worth studying is how this transformation happens: how does a new interpretation or an interpersonal connection change attention, bodily states, and choices of action, and how does this give the same situation a different meaning? The problem has not yet been solved, but our relationship to it has already begun to change.

The Subjective World Is More Than a Sensory Picture

When we talk about consciousness, it is easy to think of the scene before our eyes: a face, a patch of red, a moving object. But far more than these appears in our subjective world.

The same face is different when we see it before us and when we recall it in our mind, and different when it is familiar and when it is unfamiliar. Whether he is looking at me or at someone else also changes the experience. We experience not only facial features and contours, but also where this face comes from, whom it is connected to, and what it means to us. These relations do not have to be put into a sentence before they can become part of experience.

The subjective world also contains bodily discomfort and comfort, emotional ups and downs, memories and imagination, thoughts not yet spoken, and actions we want to take but hesitate over. “I remember something he said to me yesterday, feel hurt, want to explain, but then stop.” Such an experience is difficult to reduce to a sensory picture. Time, source, subject, emotion, and intention together organize its content.

This also changes the questions we ask when studying consciousness. Besides asking how the brain represents faces, colors, and locations, we can ask: how does the same content become something currently seen rather than remembered, the first item rather than the second, or something related to me rather than to someone else? After finding the neural representation of content, we still need to understand how these relations help shape experience.

The internal model of consciousness we hope to find is therefore not merely a set of models depicting the external world. It must also explain how the external world, the body, memory, and action are organized into a subjective world that is being experienced. This includes both “what is there” and “how these things are related, and what their relationship is to me.”

Higher Cognition: Making Complex Relations Manageable

We often describe intelligence in terms of “remembering more, remembering longer, and seeing further.” But how do these abilities connect?

I would like to propose a hypothesis: one core capacity of higher cognition is to organize complex dependencies across time and situations into structures that can be understood, reasoned through, and used with limited resources.

Imagine a monkey that must first use a short stick to reach a long stick, and then use the long stick to obtain food. The short stick in front of it has value because it is connected to a next step that has not yet been completed. Solving the problem requires linking the current action, intermediate conditions, and final goal.

Memory makes such connections possible. Remembering a little longer allows past information to inform present judgments; remembering a little more brings more objects into comparison. Our perspective, in turn, determines which relationships enter consideration. Here, perspective means not only how far we can physically see, but also how far we can mentally reach: conditions that are not before our eyes, possibilities that have not yet occurred, and the consequences of different choices.

Yet the more we take into account, the greater the processing burden may become. Objects also constrain one another: completing one thing may require another to happen first, and one choice may close off other paths. Complexity arises not only from quantity, but also from how these dependencies are organized.

This is where abstraction, chunking, and breaking problems down acquire a shared significance. Organizing a sequence of actions into a subgoal, bringing multiple details together under a rule, or temporarily setting aside differences that are irrelevant to the present task can all spare a system from processing every piece of information from scratch each time.

The key to this kind of “compression” is what gets preserved. For the goal of obtaining food, how the tools work in sequence matters, whereas some details of their surfaces may not. A good representation makes critical dependencies easier to use; an inappropriate simplification may discard precisely the conditions that determine success or failure.

Improved cognition may therefore involve two complementary directions: taking relationships across longer periods and broader situations into account, while finding more effective ways to organize them so that they remain manageable within limited memory and computational resources.

Following this hypothesis, we can ask a further question: when facing a new problem, can a system identify the crucial relationships, organize them, and reorganize them when conditions change?

This may offer one clue to understanding higher cognition: being able both to accommodate complexity and to find structures for dealing with it.

How Do Machines Know Themselves?

When an artificial intelligence says “I am thinking,” is it describing its actual state, or simply generating an appropriate response? This question can easily lead us to two extremes: we may be persuaded by fluent self-descriptions, or we may assume that nothing lies behind those words simply because the speaker is a machine. Perhaps we can begin by separating two questions: whether a system can access and use its own internal states, and whether that access is accompanied by experience.

Recent research has begun to make the first question more concrete. Researchers have tried directly altering the internal activity of language models and then observing whether the models can detect those changes. Other work has found that certain internal representations can be both reported and modulated, and can contribute to reasoning across different tasks. These experiments go beyond simply asking a machine, “Are you conscious?” We are beginning to intervene in systems and trace the relationship between their self-reports and internal processes. What can be established so far, however, is information access and functional roles under specific conditions, not that machines already have subjective experience.

What interests me is whether a system that interacts continuously with its environment will gradually form internal models of the world and of itself. It needs to distinguish changes caused by the outside world from those caused by its own actions, and may also need to estimate what it knows and does not know. Such structures might help a system exhibit more stable self-monitoring and autonomous behavior. But even if all these functions are present, we still do not know whether there is something it feels like to be that system. Findings from the human brain can offer clues for research on machines, but they cannot automatically establish that similar computations in a different substrate will produce the same experience.

This is also one of the hardest issues in current debates about machine consciousness and model welfare. Some worry that including “I may have feelings” in training will make models’ self-reports more compelling, while making it harder to judge what those reports actually show. Others argue that, under uncertainty, we should seriously investigate whether models might have interests that deserve consideration. Both concerns are worth examining. Rather than rushing to label machines “conscious” or “unconscious,” I want to know: what kinds of internal structures, interactions, and intervention results would genuinely change our judgment about this question?

Trump’s Foreign Policy: Redistributing Costs in Global Networks

Why does Trump press Europe to take greater responsibility for its security while negotiating with China and Russia and taking military action against Iran? To understand these choices, we can first consider the United States’ position in global networks, then examine how personal style and domestic politics shape its decisions.

With an ocean on either side, the United States has more room than European countries to adjust its commitments when faced with war in Europe. But alliances also provide the United States with bases, intelligence, and influence. Cutting expenditure may ease the burden, but it may also cost the cooperation needed to sustain its advantages.

The Trump administration’s National Security Strategy emphasizes shifting and sharing security burdens, calling on allies to take greater responsibility for security in their own regions. This helps explain American pressure on Europe. Asking Europe to shoulder more of the burden, however, is a different question from whether the United States is abandoning Ukraine entirely.

Russia raises questions of nuclear risk and European security. China, in addition to being a military competitor, is deeply embedded in manufacturing, critical-mineral supply chains, and trade networks. Different relationships connect different interests, calling for different forms of pressure. Competition and negotiation can take place at the same time, but this is not enough to establish the existence of a comprehensive plan to drive China and Russia apart.

The Middle East presents another tension. The United States is now a net energy exporter, yet it remains exposed to global oil prices. Allies’ security, shipping routes, and nuclear proliferation also continue to shape its choices. Military action against Iran can be analyzed within these constraints.

Similar tensions arise in the economy. The dollar’s international role depends on financial markets, demand for assets, and transaction networks, extending far beyond oil payments. The benefits the United States gains from globalization and the costs of industrial adjustment do not fall on the same groups of people. Preserving financial and market advantages, protecting manufacturing jobs, and reducing overseas commitments are goals that can conflict.

From this perspective, Biden’s cultivation of alliances and Trump’s transactional pressure can be understood as different valuations of the benefits of cooperation, immediate costs, and risks. This is only a framework for comparison: both administrations use negotiation, sanctions, and military force, and their policies are also shaped by elections, interest groups, and misjudgments.

The more interesting questions concern feedback. As allies increase their commitments, will they become more dependent on the United States or develop alternative partnerships? When rivals come under pressure, will they concede or move closer together? Global order changes through these mutual responses. Evaluating a policy requires examining the relationships it changes and whether short-term savings come at the expense of the capacity for long-term cooperation.
